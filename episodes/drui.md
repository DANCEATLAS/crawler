### 3. When it is danced (60 s) · `occasion`
NARRATION: Celebrated annually on 26 December (Saint Stephen's Day or Boxing Day, known as Lá an Dreoilín), the event historically ensured prosperity and good luck for households in the coming year.
NARRATION: Feathers from the wren were distributed to donors as protective amulets against witchcraft and supernatural harm.
NARRATION: The evening gathered funds and food to host a 'wren dance' or 'wren's wake', which functioned as a vital seasonal social event bringing unmarried young people together.
NARRATION: Today it is a living tradition.

### 7. The people (60 s) · `people`
NARRATION: Folklorist Mona Douglas recorded detailed Manx accounts of the wren pole dance and release ritual.
NARRATION: Traditional performers like sean-nós singer Seosamh Ó hÉanaí preserved oral lore and songs surrounding the tradition, while modern folk groups like Steeleye Span, Lankum, and The Chieftains have recorded wren tunes.
NARRATION: While central to Irish and Manx folklore, parallel midwinter traditions existed across Wales (Hela'r Dryw), England (such as Boxing Day dances in Suffolk), France (Fête du Roi de l'Oiseau in Puy-en-Velay and Carcassonne), and Galicia (Caceria do rei Charlo).
