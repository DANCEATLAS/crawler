## The teaching DNA so far

What a teacher does, wherever two or more kinds of evidence agree. This list grows and changes as the evidence does.

**See it**
- Show the step from behind or mirrored, so it can be copied directly. ●●● (9 teachers in 2 dance families; 50 learner comments; 3 research notes)
- Show the whole thing once at speed before breaking it down. ●● (7 teachers in 4 dance families; 0 learner comments; 2 research notes)

**Build it**
- Teach one small piece at a time and practise it before adding the next. ●●● (101 teachers in 9 dance families; 183 learner comments; 3 research notes)
- Feet first; add arms, styling and turns only when the feet are steady. ●● (41 teachers in 4 dance families; 0 learner comments; 2 research notes)
- Start slow and speed up in steps to full tempo. ●●● (26 teachers in 5 dance families; 12 learner comments; 1 research note)
- Count out loud or speak the rhythm. ●●● (43 teachers in 6 dance families; 21 learner comments; 4 research notes)
- Bring in the music early, not only at the end. ●●● (32 teachers in 5 dance families; 20 learner comments; 5 research notes)
- Repeat more than feels necessary, with follow-along rounds. ●●● (65 teachers in 8 dance families; 7 learner comments; 4 research notes)
- End with a recap and one small practice to take away. ●●● (14 teachers in 3 dance families; 6 learner comments; 5 research notes)

**Feel it**
- Give an image to aim at instead of a muscle to control. ●●● (15 teachers in 2 dance families; 29 learner comments; 3 research notes)
- Teach the feeling and the bounce before precision. ●●● (22 teachers in 4 dance families; 77 learner comments; 12 research notes)
- Invite the learner to vary it and make it their own. ●● (6 teachers in 3 dance families; 0 learner comments; 5 research notes)
- Open or close with a moment to arrive: breath, music, thanks. ●● (4 teachers in 2 dance families; 0 learner comments; 9 research notes)

**Stay with it**
- Get the learner dancing something real in the first minutes. ●●● (46 teachers in 6 dance families; 12 learner comments; 3 research notes)
- Tell learners they can do it, and name each small win. ●●● (63 teachers in 7 dance families; 19 learner comments; 11 research notes)
- Treat mistakes as normal, and show your own. ●●● (22 teachers in 7 dance families; 9 learner comments; 8 research notes)
- Bring humour and visible joy. ●●● (16 teachers in 6 dance families; 9 learner comments; 10 research notes)
- Offer choices: the tempo, the variation, when to pause. ●● (12 teachers in 3 dance families; 0 learner comments; 9 research notes)
- Connect the learner with others: a partner, a challenge, something to share. ●●● (11 teachers in 4 dance families; 3 learner comments; 18 research notes)
- Give a reason to come back: the next step, a series, a routine. ●●● (51 teachers in 8 dance families; 51 learner comments; 13 research notes)

**Fit the person**
- Name the common mistake and show the fix. ●●● (39 teachers in 6 dance families; 15 learner comments; 1 research note)
- Offer an easier version the moment a step is hard. ●● (11 teachers in 3 dance families; 2 learner comments; 6 research notes)
- Offer a harder version to those who are ready. ●● (32 teachers in 5 dance families; 1 learner comment; 8 research notes)
- Give options for different bodies, small rooms and no partner. ●●● (14 teachers in 6 dance families; 16 learner comments; 7 research notes)
- Teach learners how to check themselves. ●●● (17 teachers in 3 dance families; 11 learner comments; 6 research notes)

**Be a mentor**
- Say what the learner may be feeling, and answer it. ●● (64 teachers in 6 dance families; 0 learner comments; 6 research notes)
- Share your own beginnings and struggles. ●● (7 teachers in 3 dance families; 0 learner comments; 3 research notes)
- Say, and show, that dance belongs to everybody. ●● (38 teachers in 6 dance families; 0 learner comments; 1 research note)
- Treat the learner as a dancer from the first minute. ●● (6 teachers in 3 dance families; 0 learner comments; 4 research notes)
- Ask the learner to notice how it felt. ●● (21 teachers in 3 dance families; 0 learner comments; 5 research notes)
- Give permission to pause and to come back tomorrow. ●● (14 teachers in 3 dance families; 0 learner comments; 1 research note)

## How the Best Dance Friend adjusts

What learners say is hard, what the teachers offer for it, and what the research adds. Built only from the evidence below.

**Body adaptation** (11 comments)
- Learners say: age-related challenges like being in 50s, 60s, or 70s; having only one leg and needing to repeat the video many times
- A teacher's answer: The learner may not have access to a ballet barre for practicing fouettés. → The teacher suggests using a chair or any sturdy object to hold onto for support. ([BodyKinect by Kendall, Fouetté](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Rq0ovsiB4MQ&t=543s))
- A teacher's answer: The learner might find the arm movements repetitive. → The teacher suggests moving the arms forward, backward, or in any direction that feels natural. ([Rianna Bartuli, First steps (basics)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Rs9j8ovR5ME&t=294s))
- Research: Encourage action exploration and allow learners to test different movement variations, as motor variability can facilitate faster learning.

**Error correction** (10 comments)
- Learners say: difficulty isolating chest without moving shoulders or waist; landing both feet at the same time incorrectly; getting stuck going backwards and getting stepped on
- A teacher's answer: The teacher explains the correct knee alignment for a plié, emphasizing the importance of not overextending. ([BodyKinect by Kendall, Fouetté](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Rq0ovsiB4MQ&t=66s))
- A teacher's answer: Teacher names a common student confusion between shuffle and flap steps and demonstrates the correct execution. ([The Dance Prof, tap dance usa](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HZruz8wO0g8&t=272s))
- Research: Provide feedback after the movement is completed rather than during it to encourage independent learning.

**Feel and groove** (9 comments)
- Learners say: muscles aching quickly or feeling stiff; looking stiff; not coordinated enough to do the steps
- A teacher's answer: Performs circular hip rotations to warm up the waist area. ([АНТОН АСТАФЬЕВ УЧИТЕЛЬ ТАНЦЕВ, First steps (basics)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7VbynFspxsM&t=98s))
- A teacher's answer: The teacher instructs the learner to softly massage their face to increase oxygen levels and relax muscles. ([Tamara Nazon, Arriving and being at ease (basics)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-h1baHdSGsI&t=59s))
- Research: Incorporate full-body bouncing or vestibular motion early in the lesson to help learners physically feel and interpret the musical beat.

**Normalise mistakes** (9 comments)
- Learners say: looking silly or funny in the mirror; uncoordinated and clumsy
- A teacher's answer: Not having a natural body turnout or starting ballet late in life → Work with what you do have and make modifications along the way; imperfect turnout is totally okay ([Ballet Misfit, Ballet](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FD6bB3Mz7q0&t=30s))
- A teacher's answer: looking down at the feet → look down at the steps first to get used to where you are going, then look up later ([Bachata Dance Academy, Salsa](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jpIJMKjxkQ0&t=300s))
- Research: Praise learners specifically for their effort, persistence, and the strategies they use rather than labeling them as naturally talented.

**Imagery** (6 comments)
- Learners say: struggled for years until the skipping analogy made it click
- A teacher's answer: The teacher uses the imagery of a paintbrush in the center of the chest to guide circular movements. ([Tamara Nazon, Arriving and being at ease (basics)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-h1baHdSGsI&t=260s))
- A teacher's answer: Demonstrates the floaty texture by comparing the movement to throwing a balloon that moves fast then slows down. ([STEEZY, How teachers teach (basics)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nHZmIW6l4FM&t=210s))
- Research: Replace a list of technical body positions with a single metaphor, such as 'imagine you are a puppet being pulled by a string'.

**Counts and rhythm** (5 comments)
- Learners say: count discrepancy between quick-quick-slow-slow and standard 4-beat songs; tripped up by starting phrases on the 8; confusion about the rumba count between beats 4 and 1
- A teacher's answer: The teacher introduces counting to the side-to-side step, helping the learner internalize the rhythm. ([Oleg Astakhov, Swing](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RUCbV8C9CVE&t=142s))
- A teacher's answer: Demonstrates the first step with counts from 1 to 8, focusing on bending knees and adding flavor. ([MihranTV, Hip Hop](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ujREEgxEP7g&t=28s))
- Research: Incorporate full-body bouncing or vestibular motion early in the lesson to help learners physically feel and interpret the musical beat.

**Self check** (4 comments)
- Learners say: feeling good while dancing until recording reveals mistakes; looked stiff when watching own dance
- A teacher's answer: Uses a close-up shot of the feet to ensure the learner can see the weight distribution and foot placement clearly. ([MihranTV, The Jerk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fMEBZ59AQLk&t=63s))
- A teacher's answer: The teacher reminds the learner to check their posture, keep their back straight, neck tall, and maintain a neutral pelvis. ([Tahiti Dance Fitness, Aparima](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O7xbn7LwkfY&t=14s))
- Research: Provide feedback after the movement is completed rather than during it to encourage independent learning.

**Mirror view** (2 comments)
- Learners say: getting confused by facing opposite directions
- A teacher's answer: Explains that she will mirror movements so the learner's right side matches her left. ([FitLara Dance, Locking](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uuxFumbQ3WM&t=56s))
- A teacher's answer: The teacher turns sideways to show the footwork from a different angle. ([Social Dance Online, Waltz](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-ewZ4bWRkVo&t=129s))
- Research: Let learners watch dance sequences repeatedly to build visual familiarity and engage their motor simulation networks before they try to execute the movements.

**Music early** (2 comments)
- Learners say: difficulty keeping rhythm or getting steps right without music
- A teacher's answer: Applies all learned footwork to music in a guided warm-up drill. ([The Dance Prof, tap dance usa](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HZruz8wO0g8&t=402s))
- A teacher's answer: Practices the first part of the choreography with music at a slow tempo. ([FitLara Dance, Locking](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uuxFumbQ3WM&t=388s))
- Research: Incorporate music early in the learning process to leverage its role as a natural reward system.

**Regression** (2 comments)
- Learners say: trouble with hopping on one foot and weak feet
- A teacher's answer: Jumps and high-impact movements causing joint stress → Perform the shuffle dance without jumps, keeping movements low-impact ([АНТОН АСТАФЬЕВ УЧИТЕЛЬ ТАНЦЕВ, First steps (basics)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7VbynFspxsM&t=21s))

- Research: Encourage action exploration and allow learners to test different movement variations, as motor variability can facilitate faster learning.

**Social connection** (2 comments)
- Learners say: difficulty finding a dance partner
- A teacher's answer: The teacher greets the learners, asks how they are doing, and introduces the special training session for them. ([AGAVIK999, First steps (basics)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VO1HD_VRCvs&t=5s))
- A teacher's answer: The teacher welcomes the learner and introduces herself, creating a warm and inviting atmosphere for the session. ([Tamara Nazon, Arriving and being at ease (basics)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9DhwZqNJkIU&t=13s))
- Research: Give learners choices during the lesson to support their autonomy and intrinsic motivation.

**Tempo ramp** (2 comments)
- Learners say: hard to keep up once the speed increases
- A teacher's answer: Large amplitude hip rotations → Start with a smaller amplitude and adjust based on personal physical condition ([АНТОН АСТАФЬЕВ УЧИТЕЛЬ ТАНЦЕВ, First steps (basics)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7VbynFspxsM&t=106s))

# The evidence

## The teaching practices, strongest evidence first

### Chunking ●●●
_small pieces, each practised before the next one is added._
- Evidence: 95 teachers show it in 106 lessons across 9 dance families, 6 teachers say it in talks (82% of lessons studied); best-ranked third 87%, the others 80%; learners: 182 say it made the lesson work, 0 struggle here, 1 ask for it.
- АНТОН АСТАФЬЕВ УЧИТЕЛЬ ТАНЦЕВ (First steps (basics), [2:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7VbynFspxsM&t=135s)): Isolates leg and knee rotations to loosen up the joints.
- BodyKinect by Kendall (Fouetté, [2:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Rq0ovsiB4MQ&t=145s)): The teacher breaks down the turn into two distinct, manageable parts: a plié and a passé, encouraging students to practice this sequence repeatedly.
- Oleg Astakhov (Swing, [1:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RUCbV8C9CVE&t=70s)): The teacher breaks down the basic side-to-side step, demonstrating it slowly and clearly for the learner to follow.
- Learners (say it clicked, 15): clear and simple breakdown of steps made it easy to follow
- Learners (say it clicked, 15): clear and detailed step-by-step breakdown
- Research (cognitive load): Reducing extraneous cognitive load and keeping working memory demands low during instruction helps learners acquire motor skills and form long-term memory. → Keep instructions clear and uncluttered to avoid overloading the learner's working memory.
- Research (chunking): When learning a new sequence, breaking movements into smaller clusters or chunks reduces working memory load and improves retention and performance. → Break down long movement sequences into smaller groups of three to four elements to match optimal memory chunk sizes.

## What the research says

Each finding stands on words copied from the source and checked against it. Most studies are about learning movement in general, not dance.

### Action observation in dance
When people observe actions they already know or have seen before, their brain's mirror and motor areas simulate those movements more strongly.
- Expert dancers show greater brain activation in premotor and parietal areas when watching movements from their own trained dance style compared to unfamiliar styles. — "We found greater bilateral activations in premotor cortex and intraparietal sulcus, right superior parietal lobe and left posterior superior temporal sulcus when expert dancers viewed movements that they had been trained to perform compared to movements they had not." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/15616133))
- Brain activity during action simulation is enhanced when learners have physical experience or practice with the observed movements. — "Critically, inferior parietal lobule and ventral premotor activity was modulated as a function of dancers' ratings of their own ability to perform the observed movements and their motor experience." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/16530429))
- Observational learning alone helps build action resonance processes in the brain even without immediate physical practice. — "These imaging data demonstrate the emergence of action resonance processes in the human brain based on observational learning without physical practice and identify commonalities in the neural substrates for physical and observational learning." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/18515297))
- Visual familiarity through frequent viewing of a specific dance style can enhance motor simulation and corticospinal excitability even without physical training. — "We observed that frequent ballet spectators showed larger MEP amplitudes in the arm muscles when watching ballet compared to when they watched other performances." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/22457754))
- For the teacher: Let learners watch dance sequences repeatedly to build visual familiarity and engage their motor simulation networks before they try to execute the movements.

### Adaptive difficulty
Adjusting task difficulty dynamically based on performance helps learners improve their movements and generalise their skills.
- Motor adaptation is impaired when the executive system is heavily burdened by a difficult secondary task. — "We find that motor adaptation was specifically impaired on the most difficult to categorize trials." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/18560546))
- Adjusting task difficulty with an adaptation algorithm improves movement accuracy, velocity, and range of motion. — "The level of difficulty for the exercise was adjusted by a performance-dependent real-time adaptation algorithm." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/27895550))
- Adjusting treatment difficulty based on performance criteria helps learners generalise their skills beyond basic levels. — "Treatment difficulty was adjusted in an adaptive manner following predetermined criteria for advancing, maintaining, or moving back a level" ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/25216375))
- Self-adaptive serious games that dynamically adjust to individual performance lead to greater improvements in speed-accuracy trade-offs. — "Self-adaptive serious games, which dynamically adjust task difficulty, may provide additional benefits by tailoring challenges to individual performance." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/40021077))
- For the teacher: Dynamically adjust the difficulty of the movement practice based on how well the learner is performing in real time.

### Analogy learning
Teaching movement through metaphors or analogies allows learners to perform better under stress and multitasking because it reduces the need for conscious rule-following.
- Analogy learning results in the same level of skill as explicit instruction but with significantly fewer technical rules for the learner to remember. — "the analogy and implicit learning groups accumulated equivalently fewer explicit rules than the explicit learning group during the learning phase." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/11354610))
- Learners who use analogies maintain their performance even when distracted by a second task, unlike those taught with technical details. — "performance deteriorated for both the explicit and control conditions, but not for the analogy condition." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/19153868))
- Using metaphors for movement helps children learn complex physical skills more effectively than giving them step-by-step instructions. — "Children in the analogy protocol displayed better rope skip performance than those in the explicit instruction protocol" ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/27685604))
- Analogy learning is an effective way to teach balance and stability while keeping the learner's mind free for other tasks. — "analogy learners reported fewer explicit knowledge and demonstrated better counting backward performance than explicit learners." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/32948108))
- For the teacher: Replace a list of technical body positions with a single metaphor, such as 'imagine you are a puppet being pulled by a string'.
- For the teacher: Avoid asking students to verbalize the rules of a movement; instead, focus on the 'feeling' or the 'image' of the action.
- For the teacher: Use analogies specifically when teaching complex sequences to prevent the learner from being overwhelmed by too much information.
- Limits: The sources note that implicit learning may still require a minimal amount of attention and working memory, and some scientists debate the very existence of the concept due to the difficulty of verbalizing the knowledge gained.

### Anxiety and confidence
Performance anxiety involves both mental and physical symptoms that can be managed by helping learners feel more in control.
- Feeling out of control is a major theme in performance anxiety, and prevention or handling of anxiety can be accomplished by helping individuals feel in control. — "Feeling out of control emerged as a major theme in both the experience of anxiety and its interpretation. As a result, prevention or handling of anxiety symptoms may be accomplished by helping dancers to feel in control." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/21703084))
- Cognitive anxiety is unanimously interpreted as harmful to performance, while physical anxiety symptoms are often recognized as potentially beneficial. — "Results revealed that cognitive anxiety was more dominant than somatic anxiety, and was unanimously interpreted as debilitative to performance. Somatic anxiety was more likely to be interpreted as facilitative, with the majority of dancers recognizing that a certain amount of anxiety could be beneficial to performance." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/21703084))
- Relaxing the body with deep breaths and mentally preparing oneself are effective ways to treat performance anxiety without medication. — "Relaxing the body with deep breaths, mentally preparing oneself, and redirecting one's attention are techniques that may help with minimizing stage fright." ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stage_fright))
- For the teacher: Give learners choices during practice so they feel in control of their learning process.
- For the teacher: Guide learners through deep breaths and mental preparation before trying a challenging sequence.
- For the teacher: Help learners reframe physical nervousness as a natural and potentially helpful state of readiness.

### Autonomy support
Supporting a learner's autonomy through small choices improves skill acquisition and retention, even when those choices are entirely unrelated to the physical task.
- Providing learners with a minor choice regarding task conditions improves their performance accuracy on later retention tests. — "Autonomy support and enhanced expectancies had additive advantages for learning, with both main effects being significant for retention and transfer." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/25046402))
- Self-controlled practice choices can enhance learning outcomes without needing to provide task-relevant information or strategic advantages. — "Thus, self-controlled practice conditions can influence motor learning without providing task-relevant information, content, or strategic learning advantages." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/25732095))
- Offering choices about matters completely unrelated to the core movement task can still result in more effective learning on retention tests. — "Balance learning was significantly more effective in the choice group on a retention test." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/25732095))
- Combining autonomy support with enhanced expectancies and an external focus of attention optimizes overall learning outcomes. — "On a delayed retention test, the AS-EE-EF group outperformed all other groups." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/28056637))
- For the teacher: Allow learners to make small choices during the lesson, such as selecting the color of a virtual prop or the order of practice routines.
- For the teacher: Combine autonomy support with positive feedback regarding the learner's progress to further enhance motor learning.

### Awe and transcendence
Awe is an emotion triggered by vast or complex experiences that challenge our current understanding, leading to a diminished focus on the self and an increased sense of connection to others.
- Awe arises when we encounter stimuli that exceed our existing mental structures, requiring us to adjust our perspective. — "the emotion of awe (i.e., challenge that exceeds the scope of one's mental structures, requiring cognitive accommodation) awakens self-transcendence" ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/34855435))
- Experiencing awe can help learners feel less focused on their individual concerns and more connected to the world around them. — "When describing the experience of natural beauty, participants were more likely to report that they felt unaware of day-to-day concerns, felt the presence of something greater" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Awe))
- Awe is linked to improved well-being and a greater sense of meaning, which can foster a more positive learning environment. — "Awe involves five processes linked to well-being: "shifts in neurophysiology, a diminished focus on the self, increased prosocial relationality, greater social integration, and a heightened sense of meaning."" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Awe))
- For the teacher: Incorporate music or visual elements that evoke a sense of vastness or beauty to help students shift their focus away from day-to-day anxieties.
- For the teacher: Encourage students to reflect on the 'bigger picture' of their movement practice to foster a sense of self-transcendence and connection to the dance community.
- Limits: Awe may not be experienced if the stimulus is easily assimilated into existing knowledge or if the individual is in actual physical danger.

### Basic needs
Satisfying learners' basic psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness fosters motivation and sustained participation.

([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/42480050))
- Competence is built through skill confidence and bodily self-awareness, reinforced by peer recognition. — "Competence was reflected in skill confidence and bodily self-awareness, supported by peer recognition and opportunities to contribute to the group." - For the teacher: Build competence by highlighting skill confidence and bodily self-awareness.
- For the teacher: Foster a sense of relatedness and community connection during lessons.

### Beginners
Learning complex movement sequences and motor tasks is facilitated by repeated exposure to structured patterns and consistent practice, which leads to measurable improvements in performance and cognitive processing.
- Novice observers can implicitly learn the underlying structure or 'grammar' of dance movement sequences through simple exposure. — "Through exposure, novice observers can learn a grammar that governs the sequencing of dance movements." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/19857027))
- Training in complex motor tasks, such as dance-based video games, leads to significant increases in motor performance and changes in brain activity patterns. — "Training significantly increased the motor performance using the number of temporally accurate steps in the DVG as criteria." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/26401727))
- Dance training has been shown to provide benefits for dancers, contributing to the validation of neurofeedback and creative performance. — "A/T and HRV training have benefitted dancers." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/24239853))
- For the teacher: Expose beginners to repeated sequences of movement to help them implicitly grasp the 'grammar' or flow of the dance.
- For the teacher: Use consistent, repetitive practice of specific movement patterns to improve motor performance and accuracy.
- For the teacher: Incorporate visual and auditory cues to help learners synchronize their movements with the rhythm.
- Limits: The research does not support the claim that dance is superior to other forms of physical activity like walking for improving executive function in healthy older adults.

### Challenge point
Learning is most effective when tasks are difficult enough to require effort but still achievable, a concept known as the optimal challenge point.
- Tasks that feel difficult and require more effort often lead to better long-term performance than easier tasks that provide only temporary gains. — "Research suggests that while some approaches to learning may feel slow and difficult, the long-term benefits are greater than with easy tasks." ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desirable_difficulty))
- Allowing learners to choose their own level of task difficulty can improve their performance and accuracy in retention tests. — "The self-control condition resulted in better performances and accuracy during immediate and delayed retention tests. These results confirm the advantage of a self-control condition on motor learning." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/22428409))
- The optimal challenge point is determined by the relationship between the difficulty of the task and the learner's current skill level. — "A model called the challenge point framework can also be used. It is based on the relationship between task difficulty and the ability or the knowledge and skills of the learner." ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desirable_difficulty))
- Adapting practice conditions to individual skill levels helps maintain an appropriate level of challenge for the learner. — "One important take-away is that adaptation of the game conditions can individualize the difficulty of a game to a player's skill level in competitive multiplayer games." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/30454009))
- For the teacher: Offer the learner a choice between different difficulty levels for a dance step to increase their engagement and performance.
- For the teacher: Avoid making every practice session feel easy, as the effort required to overcome difficulty is what leads to long-term retention.
- For the teacher: Adapt the complexity of the dance movements based on the learner's current skill level to keep them at their optimal challenge point.
- Limits: Tasks that are too difficult may discourage the learner and hinder the learning process.

### Children
Structured instruction and practice in fundamental movement skills significantly improve young children's motor proficiency and broader developmental outcomes.
- Practicing fundamental movement skills through structured interventions yields significant improvements in children's movement abilities. — "preschooler's participation in the intervention resulted in greater improvement in 12 movement skills" ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/32365601))

- Fundamental movement skill practice can positively impact socio-emotional and cognitive functions in children with autism spectrum disorder. — "The FMS intervention led to improvements in both social interaction abilities and executive function in children with autism spectrum disorder." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/41039500))
- For the teacher: Incorporate regular practice of fundamental movement skills like jumping, hopping, and crossing the midline into lessons for young children.

### Chunking
When learning a new sequence, breaking movements into smaller clusters or chunks reduces working memory load and improves retention and performance.
- Working memory is relied on during learning new action sequences for chunking individual action elements together. — "they suggest that working memory is relied on during learning new action sequences for chunking individual action elements together." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/23237467))
- Individuals naturally group elementary movements into efficient memory units, or chunks, when learning a motor sequence. — "human subjects learn a visuomotor sequence by spontaneously chunking the elementary movements, while each chunk acts as a single memory unit." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/12879170))
- The striatal system plays a critical role in motor chunking and predictive stimulus-response associations. — "The striatal system learns predictive stimulus-response associations and is critical for motor chunking." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/22004979))
- For the teacher: Break down long movement sequences into smaller groups of three to four elements to match optimal memory chunk sizes.

### Coach and learner relationship
Coaches support motivation and satisfaction through democratic leadership and autonomy-supportive behaviors that build a positive relationship.
- Coaches' autonomy-supportive behaviours, structure, and involvement benefit athletes' needs and nurture intrinsic motivation. — "coaches' behaviours in the form of autonomy-supportive behaviours, provision of structure and involvement have a beneficial impact on athletes' needs for autonomy, competence and relatedness, which, in turn, nurture athletes' intrinsic motivation" ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/14626368))
- Communication strategies help enhance the quality of the coach-athlete relationship and athlete sport satisfaction. — "provide support for the practical utility of communications strategies in enhancing the quality of the coach-athlete relationship and athlete's experiences of sport satisfaction." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/31607989))
- Players may perceive the motivational climate as more performance-oriented and less mastery-oriented than coaches perceive it to be. — "players of both sexes perceived the motivational climate to be significantly more performance-oriented and significantly less mastery-oriented compared with the coaches." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/28203217))
- Democratic leadership has a direct positive influence on the coach-athlete relationship. — "The results indicated democratic leadership had a direct positive influence on the coach-athlete relationship, while autocratic leadership had no direct effect." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/36578680))
- For the teacher: Give learners choices during the lesson to support their autonomy and intrinsic motivation.
- For the teacher: Use democratic approaches that allow learners to have a voice in their practice.
- For the teacher: Communicate clearly and supportively to build a positive relationship and enhance satisfaction.

### Cognitive load
Reducing extraneous cognitive load and keeping working memory demands low during instruction helps learners acquire motor skills and form long-term memory.
- Limiting performance errors early in learning prevents hypothesis testing and reduces working memory dependence. — "It was predicted that in the absence of explicit instruction, reducing error would prevent hypothesis testing strategies and the concomitant accrual of declarative (explicit) knowledge, thereby reducing dependence on working memory resources." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/16087262))
- Action observation is less demanding in terms of cognitive load compared to motor imagery. — "The mirror neuron system appears to function more efficiently through AO training than MI, and AO is less demanding in terms of cognitive load than MI." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/31906593))
- Heavy cognitive load in complex virtual environments is associated with decreased long-term motor memory formation. — "Moreover, increased cognitive load was also related to decreased long-term motor memory formation." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/36199101))
- Complex instructional formats impose unnecessary cognitive load which can limit learning. — "They proposed that differences in performance were due to higher levels of the cognitive load imposed by the format of instruction." ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_load))
- For the teacher: Keep instructions clear and uncluttered to avoid overloading the learner's working memory.
- For the teacher: Constrain the environment early in practice to minimize errors and reduce the need for explicit hypothesis testing.
- Limits: The experience of cognitive load is not the same in everyone; the elderly, students, and children experience different and often higher amounts of cognitive load.

### Dance across life
Structured dance practice across the lifespan significantly improves physical health, balance, and cognitive function while requiring careful attention to injury prevention.
- Dancing helps improve balance, coordination, posture, and muscle strength across various ages. — "Dance also increases core strength which can improve balance, coordination, and posture (which can, in turn, reduce mechanical back pain)." ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dance_and_health))
- Structured dance interventions significantly improve body composition, blood biomarkers, and musculoskeletal function. — "Meta-analyses showed dance interventions significantly improved body composition, blood biomarkers, and musculoskeletal function." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/29270864))
- Long-term dancing interventions can improve balance abilities and hippocampal brain structures in older adults. — "Moreover, only the dancers achieved a significant increase in the balance composite score." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/28674488))
- Proper warmups, technique, and avoiding dancing through pain help prevent common injuries in training. — "Warming up and cooling down exercises are recommended before and after exercises to avoid strain, muscle pains, and possible injury." ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dance_and_health))
- For the teacher: Always lead the learners through a proper warm-up before starting movements to prevent injury.
- For the teacher: Remind learners not to push through pain and to stop and rest when needed.

### Dance pedagogy
Dance education relies on varied pedagogical approaches—from structured guidance to learner-centered reflection, self-compassion, and collaborative movement—to foster technical skill, self-expression, and well-being.
- Young children find it harder to remember complete routines, so primary dance education emphasizes listening to beats and practicing beginner steps to rhythm. — "Younger children have a harder time remembering full dance routines, so it is important that they focus more on listening to the beats of the music and practicing beginner moves to the rhythm." ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dance_education))
- Self-kindness serves as a strong positive predictor of life satisfaction in recreational dance settings. — "Self-kindness emerged as a strong positive predictor (β = 0.258, p p p = 0.029) negatively predicted life satisfaction." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/40711108))
- Learner-centered strategies like reflection, dialogue, and choice often correlate with positive cognitive, self-related, and psychosocial outcomes. — "results revealed that learner-centered features (reflection, dialog, collaboration, observation, and choice) were more often associated with more favorable cognitive, self-related, psychosocial, and implementation outcomes." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/42205960))
- Synchronized movement, collaborative choreography, and shared rhythm help foster prosocial behaviors such as peer connection, cooperation, and empathy in adolescents. — "Mechanisms such as synchronized movement, shared rhythm, and collaborative choreography facilitated these effects." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/42076930))
- For the teacher: Focus younger learners on listening to the musical beat and practicing basic moves to the rhythm rather than memorizing long routines.
- For the teacher: Integrate self-kindness training into lessons to cultivate emotional resilience and protect against self-judgment.
- For the teacher: Provide options for reflection, learner choice, and dialogue during class to support self-related and cognitive development.
- Limits: Conclusions regarding learner-centered pedagogy remain tentative due to heterogeneity in study designs, outcomes, and risk of bias across reviewed studies.

### Early success
Enhancing learner expectancies for successful performance and providing early positive experiences help build motivation and support motor learning.
- Conditions that enhance expectancies for future performance contribute to motor performance and learning by strengthening goal-action coupling. — "These include research on (a) conditions that enhance expectancies for future performance" ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/26833314))

- For the teacher: Design early exercises to ensure learners achieve immediate successful outcomes to boost confidence and expectancy.

### Embarrassment and body image
Body-oriented movement and dance interventions help improve body image, physical efficacy, and embodied awareness.
- Body dissatisfaction is less pronounced in people who exercise compared to those who do not. — "body dissatisfaction (as one important factor of negative body image) was most pronounced in the non-exercise group compared to all exercise groups" ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/33841251))
- Physical efficacy acts as a psychological mediator between exercise types like ballroom dance and body image. — "Physical efficacy, as assessed with a standardized questionnaire, mediated the difference between type of exercise (using contrasts) and body image including perceptual, cognitive, and affective body image dimensions." - Dance and movement therapy shows potential benefits for body image and emotional regulation in eating disorder populations. — "Evidence in ED populations was more limited but suggested potential benefits for body image, emotional regulation, alexithymia, and subjective well-being." - Interventions that incorporate dance and physical activity have positive effects on body image and pain intensity. — "Interventions including physical activity (N=4) (e.g. aerobic exercise, dance, Pilates) consistently showed positive effects on BI and pain intensity." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/41884678))
- For the teacher: Focus on building the learner's sense of physical efficacy and capability through movement.

### Embodied cognition
Cognition is not just a brain activity but is deeply shaped by the body's physical state, sensorimotor capacities, and active interactions with the environment.
- Cognition is fundamentally linked to the body's physical characteristics and its ability to interact with the world. — "cognition depends upon the kinds of experience that come from having a body with various sensorimotor capacities" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embodied_cognition))
- The primary purpose of cognitive processes is to facilitate effective action within the environment. — "the gaining of knowledge is a stepping stone to achieving the more immediate goal of guiding behavior in response to the system's changing surroundings." ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embodied_cognition))
- Cognitive tasks are often performed by utilizing the environment rather than relying solely on internal mental processing. — "we off-load cognitive work onto the environment" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embodied_cognition))
- For the teacher: Encourage learners to use their physical environment as a tool to support their learning process.
- For the teacher: Focus on movement as a way to build cognitive understanding rather than just memorizing abstract steps.
- For the teacher: Design exercises that require the learner to actively explore and modify their physical space to improve their performance.
- Limits: The sources note that embodied cognition is a research program with overlapping and sometimes competing theories rather than a single, well-defined unified theory.

### Embodied learning
Emodied learning relies on physical activity, multisensory experience, and bodily engagement to help learners build new skills and patterns of acting.
- Bodily activity and multisensory experience cause embodied changes that become an automatic part of expertise. — "It illustrates how multisensory experience causes embodied changes that become an automatic part of physician expertise." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/31216603))
- Kinesthetic learning is defined as the process that results in new knowledge through the involvement of body movement. — "She defined kinesthetic learning as the process that results in new knowledge (or understanding) with the involvement of the learner's body movement." ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinesthetic_learning))
- Skill memories are acquired through direct demonstration and require several repetitions. — "Skill memories are difficult to convey except by direct demonstration, may be acquired without awareness, and require several repetitions." ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinesthetic_learning))
- Interventions that include higher levels of bodily embodiment can significantly increase learner engagement and learning gains. — "Results demonstrated that the three groups that included embodiment learned significantly more than the symbols and text group on the traditional keyboard post-test." - For the teacher: Use direct physical demonstration paired with multiple repetitions to help learners build skill memories.
- For the teacher: Involve whole-body movements and active bodily engagement to support kinesthetic processing during lessons.
- Limits: Scientific studies do not support the claim that using a kinesthetic modality improves learning specifically in students who merely identify kinesthetic learning as their preferred learning style.

### Emotion and learning
Emotions and interpersonal interactions significantly influence motor learning and performance, where positive emotions correlate with peak performance and partner-assisted training fosters better outcomes than robotic training.
- Peak movement responses in a difficult task are positively correlated with positive emotions and negatively correlated with negative emotions. — "Peak frequency of movement responses was positively correlated with the frequency of positive emotions." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/27145099))
- Increasing positive emotions and down-regulating negative emotional responses may be important for learning complex tasks. — "increasing positive emotions, and perhaps down-regulating negative emotional responses, may be important for improving performance and learning a complex operant task in infancy." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/27145099))
- Partner-assisted training leads to superior motor learning progress, motivation, attention, and positive emotions compared to robot-led training. — "These findings suggest that partner-assisted training fosters stronger motivation, attention, and positive emotions, contributing to superior outcomes." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/40039203))
- Motor coordination difficulties can place strain on wellbeing and cause a range of negative emotions when unsupported. — "motor coordination difficulties impacted upon social and emotional wellbeing by placing strain on relationships, prompting bullying and exclusion, putting safety at risk and causing a range of negative emotions." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/37267374))
- For the teacher: Foster positive emotions and design interactions that encourage social connection to support better motor learning outcomes.

### Enjoyment and staying with it
Exercise enjoyment, which heavily drives continued participation, is strongly boosted by music satisfaction, positive instructor interactions, and supportive social or partner dynamics.
- Satisfaction with the music used in an exercise setting accounts for the largest share of variance in exercise enjoyment. — "satisfaction with music (21%) accounted for the most variance in exercise enjoyment" ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/17590968))
- Participants experience greater enjoyment and achievement when dancing with partners who share good rapport and compatible approaches. — "gained greater enjoyment and sense of achievement from dance classes in comparison to couples who did not enjoy dancing together" ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/28482703))
- Enjoyment is a significant predictor of out-of-class physical activity levels among participants. — "Significant effects of gender, group, enjoyment, and intensity on out-of-class weekly aerobic activity were observed" ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/27723781))
- For the teacher: Play music that learners genuinely enjoy to maximize their overall satisfaction and motivation.
- For the teacher: Foster positive social interactions and community elements within the online platform to support engagement and well-being.

### Errorless learning
Structuring practice to minimize mistakes during learning reduces errors, lowers cognitive load, and supports implicit motor acquisition.
- Errorless learning procedures drastically lower the frequency of errors made during training sessions compared to conventional methods. — "subjects trained with the errorless procedure averaged only 25 S− (errors) responses in the same number of sessions." ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Errorless_learning))
- Practicing through an errorless learning protocol results in reduced verbal-analytical and motor planning region co-activation. — "participants who practiced a golf putting task implicitly (via an errorless learning protocol) displayed less alpha2 T3-Fz co-activation than those who practiced explicitly" ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/21315795))
- Beginning a task very close and moving progressively back reduces initial errors and lowers attention demands during subsequent execution. — "Reducing errors during initial learning trials may encourage an implicit mode of learning and lower the demand for cognitive resources in subsequent performance." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/21049315))
- For the teacher: Start learners very close or at a very slow, manageable version of a movement and gradually increase the distance, speed, or difficulty so they experience virtually no errors.
- Limits: Some evidence suggests that learners given errorless training can be very slow to make a discrimination reversal, and errorless procedures may not always successfully minimize errors in certain perceptual motor tasks.

### Expectancies and autonomy
Enhanced performance expectancies and autonomy support both improve motor learning and performance, and their benefits can be additive when combined.
- Expectancy effects may be rooted in dopamine responses to anticipated positive experiences during practice. — "We describe a plausible mechanism for expectancy effects rooted in responses of dopamine to the anticipation of positive experience and temporally associated with skill practice." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/26833314))
- Providing autonomy support and enhanced expectancies independently and jointly benefit motor skill learning. — "Thus, supporting learners' need for autonomy by given them a small choice--even though it was not directly related to task performance--and enhancing their performance expectancies appeared to independently influence learning." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/25046402))
- The combination of autonomy support and enhanced expectancies yields the greatest accuracy during retention and transfer tests. — "Autonomy support and enhanced expectancies had additive advantages for learning, with both main effects being significant for retention and transfer. On both tests, the AS/EE group showed the greatest throwing accuracy." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/25046402))
- Positive affect is heightened by enhanced expectancies and predicts better transfer performance. — "Positive affect was heightened in the enhanced expectancy and enhanced expectancy groups after practice and predicted transfer performance." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/24875153))
- For the teacher: Provide learners with small choices during practice, such as selecting the color of a practice tool, to support their autonomy.
- For the teacher: Give positive social-comparative feedback to enhance learners' performance expectancies.
- For the teacher: Combine autonomy support and enhanced expectancies in lessons to maximize learning and retention accuracy.

### External focus
Directing a learner's attention to the external effects of their movements rather than to the body movements themselves enhances motor learning and performance.
- An external focus of attention on the intended movement effect helps establish efficient functional connections across brain networks that support skilled movement. — "Furthermore, we consider the influence of an external focus for the establishment of efficient functional connections across brain networks that subserve skilled movement." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/26833314))
- Increasing the physical distance between the body and the external effect of the action can further enhance learning advantages. — "The purpose of the present study was to test the hypothesis that increasing the distance between the body and the action effects might further enhance the learning advantages associated with an external focus of attention." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/12589447))
- Instructions or feedback that induce an external focus of attention are generally more effective than those prompting an internal focus. — "instructions or feedback inducing an external focus may be more effective than those with an internal focus." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/16777770))
- Providing feedback that emphasizes movement effects leads to greater task accuracy than feedback that focuses on body movements. — "External-focus feedback resulted in greater accuracy than internal-focus feedback did." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/12057890))
- For the teacher: Instruct users to focus on the external effects of their movements rather than on how their body parts are moving.
- For the teacher: Design feedback cues that direct attention further away from the user's body to promote more effective movement control.

### Feedback
Feedback is essential for motor learning, but it must be used carefully because relying too heavily on constant guidance can hinder long-term retention.
- Providing feedback after an action helps learners improve their performance and understanding. — "Knowledge of results, or sometimes immediate knowledge of results, can be used for any learning where a student (or an animal) gets information after the action." ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowledge_of_results))
- Augmented feedback from experts or technology is generally effective for enhancing motor learning. — "It is generally accepted that augmented feedback, provided by a human expert or a technical display, effectively enhances motor learning." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/23132605))
- Frequent feedback can be detrimental to learning if the learner becomes too dependent on it. — "The guidance hypothesis (Schmidt, 1991) predicts that the guiding properties of augmented feedback are beneficial for motor learning when used to reduce error, but detrimental when relied upon." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/7886280))
- Some individuals may require additional sensory information or guidance to facilitate motor learning. — "patients with PD may require more training to achieve and retain motor learning and may require additional sensory information or motor guidance in order to facilitate this learning." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/30792688))
- For the teacher: Provide feedback after the movement is completed rather than during it to encourage independent learning.
- For the teacher: Avoid giving feedback after every single repetition to prevent the learner from becoming overly dependent on your guidance.
- For the teacher: Use a mix of visual, auditory, or haptic feedback to support different learning needs.
- Limits: Findings regarding the negative effects of high-frequency feedback may not apply to individuals with specific neurological conditions, such as Parkinson's disease, who may require more consistent guidance.

### Flow
Flow is an intrinsically rewarding state of focused concentration achieved when the challenge of a task matches a person's skill level.
- Flow is characterized by complete absorption and a balance between skill and task challenge. — "In essence, flow is characterized by the complete absorption in what one does, and a resulting transformation in one's sense of time. Flow is the bonding together of action and consciousness; the state of finding a balance between a skill and how challenging that task is." ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_(psychology)))

### Gamification

### Individual differences

- For the teacher: Encourage action exploration and allow learners to test different movement variations, as motor variability can facilitate faster learning.

### Infants move to rhythm

### Motor imagery
Mentally simulating movements engages motor circuits in the brain, improving performance when combined with physical practice.

- For the teacher: Pair physical movement reps with periods of vivid internal imagery, focusing on muscle sensations and visual steps.

### Music and reward

### Overlearning

- Unlike cognitive tasks, physical tasks may show performance gains during the time between practice sessions. — "Whereas participants overlearning physical tasks increased in ability during the retention interval, participants who overlearned cognitive tasks decreased in recall ability over time." - For the teacher: Encourage students to continue practicing a movement sequence even after they have performed it correctly once.
- For the teacher: Incorporate extra repetitions of a physical movement to help solidify the skill for short-term retention.
- Limits: The effects of overlearning tend to be short-lived, and it may be an inefficient method for long-term retention compared to other strategies.
