## The teaching DNA so far

What a teacher does, wherever two or more kinds of evidence agree. This list grows and changes as the evidence does.

**See it**
- Show the step from behind or mirrored, so it can be copied directly. ●●● (8 teachers in 2 dance families; 43 learner comments; 3 research notes)
- Show the whole thing once at speed before breaking it down. ●● (5 teachers in 3 dance families; 0 learner comments; 2 research notes)

**Build it**
- Teach one small piece at a time and practise it before adding the next. ●●● (73 teachers in 8 dance families; 135 learner comments; 3 research notes)
- Feet first; add arms, styling and turns only when the feet are steady. ●● (34 teachers in 5 dance families; 0 learner comments; 2 research notes)
- Start slow and speed up in steps to full tempo. ●●● (19 teachers in 5 dance families; 12 learner comments; 1 research note)
- Count out loud or speak the rhythm. ●●● (38 teachers in 4 dance families; 17 learner comments; 4 research notes)
- Bring in the music early, not only at the end. ●●● (26 teachers in 5 dance families; 18 learner comments; 4 research notes)
- Repeat more than feels necessary, with follow-along rounds. ●●● (48 teachers in 7 dance families; 7 learner comments; 2 research notes)
- End with a recap and one small practice to take away. ●●● (11 teachers in 3 dance families; 6 learner comments; 4 research notes)

**Feel it**
- Give an image to aim at instead of a muscle to control. ●● (12 teachers in 2 dance families; 0 learner comments; 3 research notes)
- Teach the feeling and the bounce before precision. ●●● (16 teachers in 3 dance families; 71 learner comments; 10 research notes)
- Invite the learner to vary it and make it their own. ●● (5 teachers in 3 dance families; 0 learner comments; 2 research notes)
- Open or close with a moment to arrive: breath, music, thanks. ●● (4 teachers in 2 dance families; 0 learner comments; 9 research notes)

**Stay with it**
- Get the learner dancing something real in the first minutes. ●●● (31 teachers in 6 dance families; 6 learner comments; 3 research notes)
- Tell learners they can do it, and name each small win. ●●● (41 teachers in 5 dance families; 16 learner comments; 8 research notes)
- Treat mistakes as normal, and show your own. ●●● (15 teachers in 5 dance families; 4 learner comments; 7 research notes)
- Bring humour and visible joy. ●●● (10 teachers in 6 dance families; 9 learner comments; 6 research notes)
- Offer choices: the tempo, the variation, when to pause. ●● (8 teachers in 3 dance families; 0 learner comments; 8 research notes)
- Connect the learner with others: a partner, a challenge, something to share. ●● (9 teachers in 3 dance families; 2 learner comments; 8 research notes)
- Give a reason to come back: the next step, a series, a routine. ●●● (30 teachers in 6 dance families; 43 learner comments; 8 research notes)

**Fit the person**
- Name the common mistake and show the fix. ●●● (26 teachers in 5 dance families; 8 learner comments; 1 research note)
- Offer an easier version the moment a step is hard. ●● (5 teachers in 2 dance families; 0 learner comments; 6 research notes)
- Offer a harder version to those who are ready. ●● (21 teachers in 4 dance families; 0 learner comments; 6 research notes)
- Give options for different bodies, small rooms and no partner. ●●● (9 teachers in 5 dance families; 6 learner comments; 3 research notes)
- Teach learners how to check themselves. ●● (16 teachers in 3 dance families; 2 learner comments; 4 research notes)

**Be a mentor**
- Say what the learner may be feeling, and answer it. ●● (47 teachers in 6 dance families; 0 learner comments; 2 research notes)
- Share your own beginnings and struggles. ●● (5 teachers in 3 dance families; 0 learner comments; 2 research notes)
- Treat the learner as a dancer from the first minute. ●● (3 teachers in 1 dance family; 0 learner comments; 1 research note)
- Ask the learner to notice how it felt. ●● (17 teachers in 2 dance families; 0 learner comments; 2 research notes)

## How the Best Dance Friend adjusts

What learners say is hard, what the teachers offer for it, and what the research adds. Built only from the evidence below.

**Counts and rhythm** (5 comments)
- Learners say: count discrepancy between quick-quick-slow-slow and standard 4-beat songs; tripped up by starting phrases on the 8; confusion about the rumba count between beats 4 and 1
- A teacher's answer: Explains the first element called Kach, bending and rising with knees without straightening them completely. ([АНТОН АСТАФЬЕВ УЧИТЕЛЬ ТАНЦЕВ, First steps (basics)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iIZTgea3lZI&t=18s))
- A teacher's answer: Counts out loud 1, 2, 3, 4 for the initial foot movement pattern. ([Line Dance with Coral, Hustle](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EAP1E-B5qk8&t=69s))
- Research: Incorporate full-body bouncing or vestibular motion early in the lesson to help learners physically feel and interpret the musical beat.

**Feel and groove** (5 comments)
- Learners say: looking stiff; not coordinated enough to do the steps; feeling uncoordinated like a drunken giraffe
- A teacher's answer: The teacher demonstrates circular hip rotations, starting with a small amplitude and gradually increasing it based on the learner's comfort. ([АНТОН АСТАФЬЕВ УЧИТЕЛЬ ТАНЦЕВ, First steps (basics)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7VbynFspxsM&t=102s))
- A teacher's answer: The teacher starts the warm-up by stretching and moving arms to the sides. ([AGAVIK999, First steps (basics)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VO1HD_VRCvs&t=118s))
- Research: Incorporate full-body bouncing or vestibular motion early in the lesson to help learners physically feel and interpret the musical beat.

**Normalise mistakes** (4 comments)
- Learners say: uncoordinated and clumsy
- A teacher's answer: Not having a natural body turnout or starting ballet late in life → Work with what you do have and make modifications along the way; imperfect turnout is totally okay ([Ballet Misfit, Ballet](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FD6bB3Mz7q0&t=30s))
- A teacher's answer: looking down at the feet → look down at the steps first to get used to where you are going, then look up later ([Bachata Dance Academy, Salsa](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jpIJMKjxkQ0&t=300s))

**Error correction** (3 comments)

- Research: Provide feedback after the movement is completed rather than during it to encourage independent learning.

**Mirror view** (2 comments)
- Learners say: getting confused by facing opposite directions

**Music early** (2 comments)

**Social connection** (2 comments)

**Tempo ramp** (2 comments)
- Learners say: hard to keep up once the speed increases
- A teacher's answer: Amplitude of movement → Start by making small amplitude steps to get used to the movement ([АНТОН АСТАФЬЕВ УЧИТЕЛЬ ТАНЦЕВ, First steps (basics)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iIZTgea3lZI&t=214s))

**Body adaptation** (1 comment)

**Self check** (1 comment)

- Research: Provide feedback after the movement is completed rather than during it to encourage independent learning.

# The evidence

## The teaching practices, strongest evidence first

### Chunking ●●●
_small pieces, each practised before the next one is added._
- Evidence: 70 teachers show it in 79 lessons across 8 dance families, 3 teachers say it in talks (84% of lessons studied); best-ranked third 88%, the others 81%; learners: 135 say it made the lesson work, 0 struggle here, 0 ask for it.
- АНТОН АСТАФЬЕВ УЧИТЕЛЬ ТАНЦЕВ (First steps (basics), [2:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7VbynFspxsM&t=136s)): The teacher breaks down the movement by focusing on rotating the hip, practicing it on one leg before switching to the other.
- Rianna Bartuli (First steps (basics), [2:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Rs9j8ovR5ME&t=177s)): The teacher introduces the second variation by adding a second step in one direction.
- The Dance Prof (How teachers teach (basics), [0:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HZruz8wO0g8&t=12s)): Teaches the ball and heel step by lifting and dropping the ball of the foot while keeping the heel lifted.
- Learners (say it clicked, 15): clear and detailed step-by-step breakdown
- Learners (say it clicked, 12): the slow, clear step-by-step breakdown made it easy to follow and finally learn
- Research (cognitive load): Reducing extraneous cognitive load and keeping working memory demands low during instruction helps learners acquire motor skills and form long-term memory. → Keep instructions clear and uncluttered to avoid overloading the learner's working memory.
- Research (chunking): When learning a new sequence, breaking movements into smaller clusters or chunks reduces working memory load and improves retention and performance. → Break down long movement sequences into smaller groups of three to four elements to match optimal memory chunk sizes.

### Repetition ●●●
_many repetitions, loops and follow-along rounds._
- Evidence: 48 teachers show it in 51 lessons across 7 dance families (52% of lessons studied); best-ranked third 50%, the others 53%; learners: 7 say it made the lesson work, 0 struggle here, 0 ask for it.
- АНТОН АСТАФЬЕВ УЧИТЕЛЬ ТАНЦЕВ (First steps (basics), [4:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7VbynFspxsM&t=247s)): The teacher practices a cross-step movement, moving forward and backward to build coordination.
- Line Dance with Coral (Hustle, [1:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EAP1E-B5qk8&t=101s)): Reviews the first part of the dance from the top before adding new movement.
- AGAVIK999 (First steps (basics), [8:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VO1HD_VRCvs&t=530s)): The teacher repeats the side-step combination three times with counting.
- Learners (say it clicked, 4): the teacher's clear breakdown and repetition
- Learners (say it clicked, 2): repeating every move made it easy to follow and learn
- Research (Evolution of Neuroplasticity in Response to Physical Activity in Old A): Dance programs that prioritize the continuous learning of new movement patterns are more effective at promoting brain health in older adults than repetitive physical exercise. → Prioritize teaching new, varied movement patterns rather than relying on repetitive drills.
- Research (Sensorimotor integration is enhanced in dancers and musicians): Long-term intensive training in dance or music leads to distinct enhancements in sensorimotor skills tailored to the specific domain. → Include whole-body imitation tasks to leverage and develop sensorimotor integration skills in learners.

## What the research says

Each finding stands on words copied from the source and checked against it. Most studies are about learning movement in general, not dance.

### Action observation in dance
When people observe actions they already know or have seen before, their brain's mirror and motor areas simulate those movements more strongly.
- Expert dancers show greater brain activation in premotor and parietal areas when watching movements from their own trained dance style compared to unfamiliar styles. — "We found greater bilateral activations in premotor cortex and intraparietal sulcus, right superior parietal lobe and left posterior superior temporal sulcus when expert dancers viewed movements that they had been trained to perform compared to movements they had not." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/15616133))
- Brain activity during action simulation is enhanced when learners have physical experience or practice with the observed movements. — "Critically, inferior parietal lobule and ventral premotor activity was modulated as a function of dancers' ratings of their own ability to perform the observed movements and their motor experience." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/16530429))
- Observational learning alone helps build action resonance processes in the brain even without immediate physical practice. — "These imaging data demonstrate the emergence of action resonance processes in the human brain based on observational learning without physical practice and identify commonalities in the neural substrates for physical and observational learning." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/18515297))
- Visual familiarity through frequent viewing of a specific dance style can enhance motor simulation and corticospinal excitability even without physical training. — "We observed that frequent ballet spectators showed larger MEP amplitudes in the arm muscles when watching ballet compared to when they watched other performances." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/22457754))
- For the teacher: Let learners watch dance sequences repeatedly to build visual familiarity and engage their motor simulation networks before they try to execute the movements.

### Adaptive difficulty
Adjusting task difficulty dynamically based on performance helps learners improve their movements and generalise their skills.
- Motor adaptation is impaired when the executive system is heavily burdened by a difficult secondary task. — "We find that motor adaptation was specifically impaired on the most difficult to categorize trials." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/18560546))
- Adjusting task difficulty with an adaptation algorithm improves movement accuracy, velocity, and range of motion. — "The level of difficulty for the exercise was adjusted by a performance-dependent real-time adaptation algorithm." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/27895550))
- Adjusting treatment difficulty based on performance criteria helps learners generalise their skills beyond basic levels. — "Treatment difficulty was adjusted in an adaptive manner following predetermined criteria for advancing, maintaining, or moving back a level" ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/25216375))
- Self-adaptive serious games that dynamically adjust to individual performance lead to greater improvements in speed-accuracy trade-offs. — "Self-adaptive serious games, which dynamically adjust task difficulty, may provide additional benefits by tailoring challenges to individual performance." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/40021077))
- For the teacher: Dynamically adjust the difficulty of the movement practice based on how well the learner is performing in real time.

### Analogy learning
Teaching movement through metaphors or analogies allows learners to perform better under stress and multitasking because it reduces the need for conscious rule-following.
- Analogy learning results in the same level of skill as explicit instruction but with significantly fewer technical rules for the learner to remember. — "the analogy and implicit learning groups accumulated equivalently fewer explicit rules than the explicit learning group during the learning phase." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/11354610))
- Learners who use analogies maintain their performance even when distracted by a second task, unlike those taught with technical details. — "performance deteriorated for both the explicit and control conditions, but not for the analogy condition." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/19153868))
- Using metaphors for movement helps children learn complex physical skills more effectively than giving them step-by-step instructions. — "Children in the analogy protocol displayed better rope skip performance than those in the explicit instruction protocol" ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/27685604))
- Analogy learning is an effective way to teach balance and stability while keeping the learner's mind free for other tasks. — "analogy learners reported fewer explicit knowledge and demonstrated better counting backward performance than explicit learners." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/32948108))
- For the teacher: Replace a list of technical body positions with a single metaphor, such as 'imagine you are a puppet being pulled by a string'.
- For the teacher: Avoid asking students to verbalize the rules of a movement; instead, focus on the 'feeling' or the 'image' of the action.
- For the teacher: Use analogies specifically when teaching complex sequences to prevent the learner from being overwhelmed by too much information.
- Limits: The sources note that implicit learning may still require a minimal amount of attention and working memory, and some scientists debate the very existence of the concept due to the difficulty of verbalizing the knowledge gained.

### Autonomy support
Supporting a learner's autonomy through small choices improves skill acquisition and retention, even when those choices are entirely unrelated to the physical task.
- Providing learners with a minor choice regarding task conditions improves their performance accuracy on later retention tests. — "Autonomy support and enhanced expectancies had additive advantages for learning, with both main effects being significant for retention and transfer." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/25046402))
- Self-controlled practice choices can enhance learning outcomes without needing to provide task-relevant information or strategic advantages. — "Thus, self-controlled practice conditions can influence motor learning without providing task-relevant information, content, or strategic learning advantages." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/25732095))
- Offering choices about matters completely unrelated to the core movement task can still result in more effective learning on retention tests. — "Balance learning was significantly more effective in the choice group on a retention test." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/25732095))
- Combining autonomy support with enhanced expectancies and an external focus of attention optimizes overall learning outcomes. — "On a delayed retention test, the AS-EE-EF group outperformed all other groups." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/28056637))
- For the teacher: Allow learners to make small choices during the lesson, such as selecting the color of a virtual prop or the order of practice routines.
- For the teacher: Combine autonomy support with positive feedback regarding the learner's progress to further enhance motor learning.

### Awe and transcendence
Awe is an emotion triggered by vast or complex experiences that challenge our current understanding, leading to a diminished focus on the self and an increased sense of connection to others.
- Awe arises when we encounter stimuli that exceed our existing mental structures, requiring us to adjust our perspective. — "the emotion of awe (i.e., challenge that exceeds the scope of one's mental structures, requiring cognitive accommodation) awakens self-transcendence" ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/34855435))
- Experiencing awe can help learners feel less focused on their individual concerns and more connected to the world around them. — "When describing the experience of natural beauty, participants were more likely to report that they felt unaware of day-to-day concerns, felt the presence of something greater" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Awe))
- Awe is linked to improved well-being and a greater sense of meaning, which can foster a more positive learning environment. — "Awe involves five processes linked to well-being: "shifts in neurophysiology, a diminished focus on the self, increased prosocial relationality, greater social integration, and a heightened sense of meaning."" ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Awe))
- For the teacher: Incorporate music or visual elements that evoke a sense of vastness or beauty to help students shift their focus away from day-to-day anxieties.
- For the teacher: Encourage students to reflect on the 'bigger picture' of their movement practice to foster a sense of self-transcendence and connection to the dance community.
- Limits: Awe may not be experienced if the stimulus is easily assimilated into existing knowledge or if the individual is in actual physical danger.

### Basic needs
Satisfying learners' basic psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness fosters motivation and sustained participation.
- Lindy Hop participation helps satisfy the basic psychological needs required for sustained dance motivation. — "satisfy the 3 basic psychological needs required for internalized motivation that contributes to sustained dance participation." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/43010569))
- Older adults experience autonomy through lifelong passion, continuous learning, and self-directed engagement. — "Participants experienced autonomy through self-directed engagement in dance, including lifelong passion, ongoing learning, and leadership within dance groups." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/42480050))
- Competence is built through skill confidence and bodily self-awareness, reinforced by peer recognition. — "Competence was reflected in skill confidence and bodily self-awareness, supported by peer recognition and opportunities to contribute to the group." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/42480050))
- For the teacher: Give learners choices in their practice to support their sense of autonomy.
- For the teacher: Build competence by highlighting skill confidence and bodily self-awareness.
- For the teacher: Foster a sense of relatedness and community connection during lessons.

### Beginners
Learning complex movement sequences and motor tasks is facilitated by repeated exposure to structured patterns and consistent practice, which leads to measurable improvements in performance and cognitive processing.
- Novice observers can implicitly learn the underlying structure or 'grammar' of dance movement sequences through simple exposure. — "Through exposure, novice observers can learn a grammar that governs the sequencing of dance movements." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/19857027))
- Training in complex motor tasks, such as dance-based video games, leads to significant increases in motor performance and changes in brain activity patterns. — "Training significantly increased the motor performance using the number of temporally accurate steps in the DVG as criteria." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/26401727))
- Dance training has been shown to provide benefits for dancers, contributing to the validation of neurofeedback and creative performance. — "A/T and HRV training have benefitted dancers." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/24239853))
- For the teacher: Expose beginners to repeated sequences of movement to help them implicitly grasp the 'grammar' or flow of the dance.
- For the teacher: Use consistent, repetitive practice of specific movement patterns to improve motor performance and accuracy.
- For the teacher: Incorporate visual and auditory cues to help learners synchronize their movements with the rhythm.
- Limits: The research does not support the claim that dance is superior to other forms of physical activity like walking for improving executive function in healthy older adults.

### Challenge point
Learning is most effective when tasks are difficult enough to require effort but still achievable, a concept known as the optimal challenge point.
- Tasks that feel difficult and require more effort often lead to better long-term performance than easier tasks that provide only temporary gains. — "Research suggests that while some approaches to learning may feel slow and difficult, the long-term benefits are greater than with easy tasks." ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desirable_difficulty))
- Allowing learners to choose their own level of task difficulty can improve their performance and accuracy in retention tests. — "The self-control condition resulted in better performances and accuracy during immediate and delayed retention tests. These results confirm the advantage of a self-control condition on motor learning." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/22428409))
- The optimal challenge point is determined by the relationship between the difficulty of the task and the learner's current skill level. — "A model called the challenge point framework can also be used. It is based on the relationship between task difficulty and the ability or the knowledge and skills of the learner." ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desirable_difficulty))
- Adapting practice conditions to individual skill levels helps maintain an appropriate level of challenge for the learner. — "One important take-away is that adaptation of the game conditions can individualize the difficulty of a game to a player's skill level in competitive multiplayer games." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/30454009))
- For the teacher: Offer the learner a choice between different difficulty levels for a dance step to increase their engagement and performance.
- For the teacher: Avoid making every practice session feel easy, as the effort required to overcome difficulty is what leads to long-term retention.
- For the teacher: Adapt the complexity of the dance movements based on the learner's current skill level to keep them at their optimal challenge point.
- Limits: Tasks that are too difficult may discourage the learner and hinder the learning process.

### Chunking
When learning a new sequence, breaking movements into smaller clusters or chunks reduces working memory load and improves retention and performance.
- Working memory is relied on during learning new action sequences for chunking individual action elements together. — "they suggest that working memory is relied on during learning new action sequences for chunking individual action elements together." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/23237467))
- Individuals naturally group elementary movements into efficient memory units, or chunks, when learning a motor sequence. — "human subjects learn a visuomotor sequence by spontaneously chunking the elementary movements, while each chunk acts as a single memory unit." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/12879170))
- The striatal system plays a critical role in motor chunking and predictive stimulus-response associations. — "The striatal system learns predictive stimulus-response associations and is critical for motor chunking." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/22004979))
- For the teacher: Break down long movement sequences into smaller groups of three to four elements to match optimal memory chunk sizes.

### Cognitive load
Reducing extraneous cognitive load and keeping working memory demands low during instruction helps learners acquire motor skills and form long-term memory.
- Limiting performance errors early in learning prevents hypothesis testing and reduces working memory dependence. — "It was predicted that in the absence of explicit instruction, reducing error would prevent hypothesis testing strategies and the concomitant accrual of declarative (explicit) knowledge, thereby reducing dependence on working memory resources." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/16087262))
- Action observation is less demanding in terms of cognitive load compared to motor imagery. — "The mirror neuron system appears to function more efficiently through AO training than MI, and AO is less demanding in terms of cognitive load than MI." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/31906593))
- Heavy cognitive load in complex virtual environments is associated with decreased long-term motor memory formation. — "Moreover, increased cognitive load was also related to decreased long-term motor memory formation." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/36199101))
- Complex instructional formats impose unnecessary cognitive load which can limit learning. — "They proposed that differences in performance were due to higher levels of the cognitive load imposed by the format of instruction." ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_load))
- For the teacher: Keep instructions clear and uncluttered to avoid overloading the learner's working memory.
- For the teacher: Constrain the environment early in practice to minimize errors and reduce the need for explicit hypothesis testing.
- Limits: The experience of cognitive load is not the same in everyone; the elderly, students, and children experience different and often higher amounts of cognitive load.

- Synchronized movement, collaborative choreography, and shared rhythm help foster prosocial behaviors such as peer connection, cooperation, and empathy in adolescents. — "Mechanisms such as synchronized movement, shared rhythm, and collaborative choreography facilitated these effects." - For the teacher: Focus younger learners on listening to the musical beat and practicing basic moves to the rhythm rather than memorizing long routines.

- For the teacher: Provide options for reflection, learner choice, and dialogue during class to support self-related and cognitive development.
- Limits: Conclusions regarding learner-centered pedagogy remain tentative due to heterogeneity in study designs, outcomes, and risk of bias across reviewed studies.

Enhancing learner expectancies for successful performance and providing early positive experiences help build motivation and support motor learning.

### Embodied learning
Emodied learning relies on physical activity, multisensory experience, and bodily engagement to help learners build new skills and patterns of acting.

### Enjoyment and staying with it

- Enjoyment is a significant predictor of out-of-class physical activity levels among participants. — "Significant effects of gender, group, enjoyment, and intensity on out-of-class weekly aerobic activity were observed" ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/27723781))
- For the teacher: Play music that learners genuinely enjoy to maximize their overall satisfaction and motivation.
- For the teacher: Foster positive social interactions and community elements within the online platform to support engagement and well-being.

### Errorless learning
Structuring practice to minimize mistakes during learning reduces errors, lowers cognitive load, and supports implicit motor acquisition.
- Errorless learning procedures drastically lower the frequency of errors made during training sessions compared to conventional methods. — "subjects trained with the errorless procedure averaged only 25 S− (errors) responses in the same number of sessions." ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Errorless_learning))
- Practicing through an errorless learning protocol results in reduced verbal-analytical and motor planning region co-activation. — "participants who practiced a golf putting task implicitly (via an errorless learning protocol) displayed less alpha2 T3-Fz co-activation than those who practiced explicitly" ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/21315795))
- Beginning a task very close and moving progressively back reduces initial errors and lowers attention demands during subsequent execution. — "Reducing errors during initial learning trials may encourage an implicit mode of learning and lower the demand for cognitive resources in subsequent performance." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/21049315))
- For the teacher: Start learners very close or at a very slow, manageable version of a movement and gradually increase the distance, speed, or difficulty so they experience virtually no errors.
- Limits: Some evidence suggests that learners given errorless training can be very slow to make a discrimination reversal, and errorless procedures may not always successfully minimize errors in certain perceptual motor tasks.

### Expectancies and autonomy
Enhanced performance expectancies and autonomy support both improve motor learning and performance, and their benefits can be additive when combined.
- Expectancy effects may be rooted in dopamine responses to anticipated positive experiences during practice. — "We describe a plausible mechanism for expectancy effects rooted in responses of dopamine to the anticipation of positive experience and temporally associated with skill practice." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/26833314))
- Providing autonomy support and enhanced expectancies independently and jointly benefit motor skill learning. — "Thus, supporting learners' need for autonomy by given them a small choice--even though it was not directly related to task performance--and enhancing their performance expectancies appeared to independently influence learning." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/25046402))
- The combination of autonomy support and enhanced expectancies yields the greatest accuracy during retention and transfer tests. — "Autonomy support and enhanced expectancies had additive advantages for learning, with both main effects being significant for retention and transfer. On both tests, the AS/EE group showed the greatest throwing accuracy." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/25046402))
- Positive affect is heightened by enhanced expectancies and predicts better transfer performance. — "Positive affect was heightened in the enhanced expectancy and enhanced expectancy groups after practice and predicted transfer performance." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/24875153))
- For the teacher: Provide learners with small choices during practice, such as selecting the color of a practice tool, to support their autonomy.
- For the teacher: Give positive social-comparative feedback to enhance learners' performance expectancies.
- For the teacher: Combine autonomy support and enhanced expectancies in lessons to maximize learning and retention accuracy.

### External focus
Directing a learner's attention to the external effects of their movements rather than to the body movements themselves enhances motor learning and performance.
- An external focus of attention on the intended movement effect helps establish efficient functional connections across brain networks that support skilled movement. — "Furthermore, we consider the influence of an external focus for the establishment of efficient functional connections across brain networks that subserve skilled movement." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/26833314))
- Increasing the physical distance between the body and the external effect of the action can further enhance learning advantages. — "The purpose of the present study was to test the hypothesis that increasing the distance between the body and the action effects might further enhance the learning advantages associated with an external focus of attention." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/12589447))
- Instructions or feedback that induce an external focus of attention are generally more effective than those prompting an internal focus. — "instructions or feedback inducing an external focus may be more effective than those with an internal focus." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/16777770))
- Providing feedback that emphasizes movement effects leads to greater task accuracy than feedback that focuses on body movements. — "External-focus feedback resulted in greater accuracy than internal-focus feedback did." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/12057890))
- For the teacher: Instruct users to focus on the external effects of their movements rather than on how their body parts are moving.
- For the teacher: Design feedback cues that direct attention further away from the user's body to promote more effective movement control.

### Feedback
Feedback is essential for motor learning, but it must be used carefully because relying too heavily on constant guidance can hinder long-term retention.
- Providing feedback after an action helps learners improve their performance and understanding. — "Knowledge of results, or sometimes immediate knowledge of results, can be used for any learning where a student (or an animal) gets information after the action." ([source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowledge_of_results))
- Augmented feedback from experts or technology is generally effective for enhancing motor learning. — "It is generally accepted that augmented feedback, provided by a human expert or a technical display, effectively enhances motor learning." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/23132605))
- Frequent feedback can be detrimental to learning if the learner becomes too dependent on it. — "The guidance hypothesis (Schmidt, 1991) predicts that the guiding properties of augmented feedback are beneficial for motor learning when used to reduce error, but detrimental when relied upon." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/7886280))
- Some individuals may require additional sensory information or guidance to facilitate motor learning. — "patients with PD may require more training to achieve and retain motor learning and may require additional sensory information or motor guidance in order to facilitate this learning." ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/30792688))
- For the teacher: Provide feedback after the movement is completed rather than during it to encourage independent learning.
- For the teacher: Avoid giving feedback after every single repetition to prevent the learner from becoming overly dependent on your guidance.
- For the teacher: Use a mix of visual, auditory, or haptic feedback to support different learning needs.
- Limits: Findings regarding the negative effects of high-frequency feedback may not apply to individuals with specific neurological conditions, such as Parkinson's disease, who may require more consistent guidance.

### Groove

- For the teacher: Use music with medium syncopation and strong groove elements to naturally elicit a desire to move in learners.

### Individual differences

- For the teacher: Encourage action exploration and allow learners to test different movement variations, as motor variability can facilitate faster learning.
- For the teacher: Break down movement sequences into smaller pieces using chunking to support learners' working memory capacity.

### Mirrors in dance

### Motor imagery
Mentally simulating movements engages motor circuits in the brain, improving performance when combined with physical practice.
- Combining mental practice with physical practice improves skill learning equal to or better than physical practice alone. — "In some medical, musical, and athletic contexts, when paired with physical rehearsal, mental rehearsal can be as effective as pure physical rehearsal (practice) of an action." - For the teacher: Guide students through a brief mental rehearsal of a movement phrase before physically executing it.
- For the teacher: Pair physical movement reps with periods of vivid internal imagery, focusing on muscle sensations and visual steps.

### Music and reward
Music acts as a powerful abstract reward that triggers dopamine release, which in turn drives motivation and the pleasure experienced during movement.
- Music triggers dopamine release in the brain's reward system, which is essential for experiencing pleasure and motivation. — "Specifically, the dorsal and ventral striatum release dopamine when listening to pleasurable music, and activity in these structures also codes the reward value of musical excerpts." - Dopamine levels directly influence how much pleasure and motivation a person derives from listening to music. — "we demonstrate that levodopa and risperidone led to opposite effects in measures of musical pleasure and motivation: while the dopamine precursor levodopa, compared with placebo, increased the hedonic experience" ([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/30670642))

([source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/21217764))
- For the teacher: Incorporate music early in the learning process to leverage its role as a natural reward system.
- For the teacher: Build anticipation for the music or the movement phrase to engage the learner's dopaminergic system before the peak of the activity.
- For the teacher: Use music that the learner finds personally pleasurable to maximize their motivation and hedonic experience.
- Limits: Approximately 5% of the population may experience 'specific music anhedonia,' where they do not derive pleasure from music despite normal perception abilities.

- For the teacher: Design lessons that run for one hour, scheduled twice a week for optimal mood benefits.
- For the teacher: Provide regular, ongoing dance sessions to help prevent the diminishing of positive emotional effects over time.
